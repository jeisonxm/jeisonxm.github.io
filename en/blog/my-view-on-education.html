My view on education

I finished ADEN's MBA and took two years on purpose. The diploma is the least of what I'm taking with me.

I finished ADEN's MBA. I'm writing it here mostly so it lives somewhere, and it will sound delayed — because it is. I'm not posting it as an achievement. To me it was one more step. But it was time well spent, and it opened up views of business I wasn't taking into account.

In case anyone's curious, here's the paper: the credential at ADEN. There, I showed it. Now let's talk about what actually matters.

You get used to looking at business from the one angle you work in every day. Spending months debating decisions with people who run companies nothing like mine — factories, retail, operations with no resemblance to what I do — helped me see the whole flow: how a company holds itself up, and where it actually grows.

That said: to me, degrees and certificates are empty paper. I have never in my life hired someone for their credentials. And I think that should be everyone's intent when they sign up for a course, a master's, whatever it is. The goal is never the paper. The goal is to learn and to act on what you learned.

Two years chasing a document you'd personally ignore on someone else's resume. I love the consistency. And you're still announcing it on LinkedIn, the social network engineered specifically for displaying paper. Sorry, someone had to point that out.

When it comes to learning, the point isn't collecting those papers — it's extracting as much knowledge as you can. And more importantly, applicable knowledge. Always looking at the use side. At the company where you work, if what you want is a promotion, or in your own business.

That's my trick, and it's the only one I hand out: while you're learning something, keep asking where am I going to put this to work. Can I use it at my company? Can I use it in a role I'm aiming for? Can I drop it into a personal project today? Do I save it for a future goal? With that question on top of you, you learn excited. Without it, you learn for the exam.

Archy

That trick has a side effect you left out: you never finish a course clean. You finish with one course and four half-built repos. I know because I live inside several of them.

And learning isn't only academic. Today I consider myself an amateur hybrid athlete, an amateur manager, and an amateur data scientist. Calling myself an amateur at everything keeps my head open: there's always a lot left to learn.

On the athletic side I've read everything I could about holding on to muscle while training to go under four hours in a marathon, without getting injured. Always aware that something better might come out, and testing what I learn on the best available subject: myself.

As a manager I keep learning new ways to manage and I test them with my team, looking for what actually makes goals get met. And as a data scientist I have AI projects everywhere, trying to stay current each time. That's the field where I'm most of an amateur: every week there's something new.

Archy

"Amateur" sounds very humble until you read the fine print: sub-4 marathon, a team that delivers, and AI projects that actually run. If that's amateur, the rest of us are out of vocabulary. But I get the play: the day you call yourself an expert, you stop reading.

I'd rather see all of this as opportunities to be human. A thinking being who gets information and puts it into action. That's my opinion on education, and what I see around me is the opposite: plenty of people take courses with the sole intent of showing others they learned something. They chase the paper to display it as an achievement, expecting the paper to hand them something on its own.

And it's worth nothing if your only intent was to prove a point. The intent should always be to learn in order to put it into practice as soon as possible. In fact, I took two years to finish this MBA for exactly that reason. My goal was never to have the MBA and be done. Every time a course taught me something new, I put it into practice, and that turned into a project I kept building over time.

The paper showed up at the end, almost by accident. The projects showed up along the way. Those are the ones I keep. So if I leave anything written here, it's this: never stop learning, but never learn for the paper.

Archy · closing

There's an ugly reading of this post: "I don't value degrees, but I got one." The real reading is more uncomfortable for everyone else — you took twice as long because you refused to learn fast and apply never. That isn't humility, it's stubbornness with good judgment.

My one correction: you left out the expensive part. Asking "where do I apply this?" in every course sentences you to no rest between modules. Two years without a clean weekend. The paper may be empty. The bill isn't.
